# Walk a Cavendish classroom

Stimpunks.World has built a classroom you can walk into: [Cavendish Primary Classroom](https://stimpunks.world/cavendish-primary-classroom). It is a mainstream primary classroom for a class of thirty, set up the Cavendish Space way, as an example for people training to teach. Every choice in it is written down with its reason and its source.

>> The room does the adjusting. Never the child.

It is one example, not a specification and not the only right answer. Take it into your placement and argue with it.

[Walk the classroom →](https://stimpunks.world/cavendish-primary-classroom)

## What you see when you walk in

The room is drawn from the height of a child of seven: the tables and the shelf at eye level, the garden through doors glazed to the floor. Nobody is drawn in it, because the room is set up for whoever comes in.

Under the picture are the room's own controls: the lamps, the blinds, the fan, and the lights in the cave. They are the settings the class has, and you can change them. The same room is on [Cel Street](https://stimpunks.world/cel-street#in=cavendish-primary-classroom), where it opens in a VR headset with every setting on the headset's panel.

## Five places in one room

The room is zoned into the five places, so anybody can move to the one that fits how they are right now. Moving between them is a skill, never misbehavior.

- **The cave** — a sensory cave in the far corner, a den under the round table, and a tent in the garden. Somewhere to be alone.
- **The campfire** — a small circle on the rug. A few people and one story, or one question.
- **The watering hole** — the tables and the tray of loose parts. Peers showing each other what they know, joined or just listened to.
- **The library** — the low shelf, the picture timetable, and a box of instructions everybody adds to. What the class has worked out, kept where anybody can reach it.
- **The habitat** — the whole room and the garden, which hold the other four. The class can change it.

[What is a Cavendish Space?](space.html) has the model; [Zone a room](rooms.html) does the same zoning for a room of your own.

## What the class can change

- **Light.** Daylight first. Lamps on a dimmer that does not flicker or hum, set to what the most light-sensitive child needs, with a task lamp for whoever needs more. Walls left mostly bare.
- **Warmth and air.** A thermometer the class can see. Windows, a slow fan, a warmer corner and a cooler one, layers on and off, water in reach all day without asking. The page says plainly that most schools run the heating for the whole building, and does not pretend otherwise.
- **Sound.** Soft surfaces so the room does not ring. Headphones on a rail by the cave, there for anybody, with no reason needed. No bells or shouted countdowns.
- **Somewhere to be.** More places to be than there are children: standing desks, chairs at more than one height, wobble stools, cushions, the floor. Room for a wheelchair everywhere. Nobody is told to sit still.
- **Less, and more.** Sensory avoiders get the dimmer, the headphones and the caves. Sensory seekers get a movement corner, heavy work that is a real job, things to chew and fiddle with, and the garden for loud and fast. Many children need both, on different days.

These are the deck's What helps cards built into a room before anybody has to ask: *dim the light*, *headphones*, *the right temperature*, *sit my way*, *room to move*, *let me stim*, *something to chew*, *less to look at*.

## The cave is never a time-out

The cave is a cloth over a frame, with cushions, a blanket and a string of small lights switched from inside. Anybody can go in, at any time, and come out when they are ready. Nobody is sent there and nobody is fetched out. It is never locked, never a consequence, and never where a child who is upset gets put. Going in early is the point.

One cave is not enough for thirty, so the room has three. The page carries the same idea out to the whole school: caves, campfires and watering holes in the library, the hall, the dining hall and the playground, and a cave for the grown-ups too.

## The day bends too

A room that changes needs a day that can. The page sets out an example day with fixed anchors — arriving, lunch, going home — and a middle that flexes: a soft start, a campfire to plan the day, learning blocks with a choice of where and how to work, an interest-led project nobody is moved on from mid-flow, and a campfire at the close about what to change in the room tomorrow. A child who needs the same order every day can have it.

## What the classroom will not do

- No behavior chart, traffic light or clip chart, on the wall or on a screen.
- No taking away play, breaks, the garden or the cave.
- No rewards for sitting still, looking at the speaker, or hiding how you feel.
- No asking permission to drink, to move, to go to the toilet or into the cave.
- No seclusion, and no label or diagnosis needed before something is made easier.

And it says what a room cannot do on its own. A behavior policy, a timetable or an assessment rule can undo everything on the page, so the rest of the school has to agree with it.

## Where the deck sits in it

The deck lies on the library shelf. A child shows how they feel and what they need by pointing at a picture or turning a card face up — no reading, no scoring, no right answer. [Place Explorers](place-explorers.html) is the five places in words for children of about seven to eleven, and [the interaction badges](badges.html) say whether somebody would like to be talked to. The page lists the project's ethics as the wall of a classroom would carry them, each linked to the page here that says it.

## Read the research the way the page does

The page gathers the research on classroom design — light, temperature, air, ownership, flexibility, how busy the walls are, places of solitude — and then says what it does not prove. A share of the variation across many classrooms is not a promise about one child. Neither headline study was of Autistic or neurodivergent pupils. **So the best evidence for any classroom is its own children:** notice who uses which place, ask, and change the room. That is what a [cogen](cogens.html) is for.

## Questions to take on placement

The page ends with questions for any classroom you walk into, including your own. They are for noticing, never for marking anybody. Three of them, in short:

- Where would a child go here to be on their own, without asking and without it counting against them?
- Is there anything on a wall or a screen that ranks children where the class can see it?
- When two children need opposite things, how is that talked through, and who is in the conversation?

The rest are on the page.
