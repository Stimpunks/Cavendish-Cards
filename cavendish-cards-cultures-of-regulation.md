# Building Neurodivergent-Affirming Cultures of Regulation

On January 11, 2026, Helen Edgar of [Autistic Realms](https://autisticrealms.com/) ran a Cavendish Space workshop for the Stimpunks community, online. It did not only talk about regulation. It practiced it — moving through a campfire, a watering hole and a cave, and making something with your hands on the way.

Helen wrote it up on stimpunks.org: [Building Neurodivergent-Affirming Cultures of Regulation: Reflections from a Cavendish Space Workshop](https://stimpunks.org/2026/03/05/building-neurodivergent-affirming-cultures-of-regulation-reflections-from-a-cavendish-space-workshop/). This page says what the workshop did, so you can run one. [The slides are here too](cultures-of-regulation-slides.pdf).

>> Regulation lives in the room and the relationships. Not in one person, holding still.

The workshop grew out of a research project, *Building Neurodivergent-Affirming Cultures of Regulation: A Neurodivergent Perspective*, led by Dr Amy Skinner of York St John University and Kay Louise Aldred of [Neurodiverse Connection](https://ndconnection.co.uk/). It asks how Neurodivergent people experience regulation, using creative and collaborative methods. [The call for participants](https://ndconnection.co.uk/blog/call-for-participants) says more.

## Everything was optional

Before anything else, the session said so. Move, stim, rest, listen, type, speak. Camera on or off. Chat, text or mic. Take part in whatever way your bodymind needs right now.

That was not an accessibility add-on. It is how every Stimpunks space runs, and it is the first thing to copy.

## What regulation means here

Not "being calm" on your own. In this workshop, regulation is:

- **Embodied** — it happens in a body, not only in a head.
- **Sensory** — it depends on what the room sounds, looks and feels like.
- **Relational** — it happens between people, and through them.
- **Environmental** — the room can make it possible, or rule it out.
- **Interdependent** — nobody does it alone, and nobody should have to.

It turns on whether you feel safe in your body, whether your sensory needs are met, whether the way you communicate is respected, and whether your nervous system is allowed to move instead of being held in vigilance or suppression. Neuronormative spaces take that agency away. Helen draws the next step from Kay Aldred's book [*Embodied Education*](https://thegirlgod.com/embodied_education.php):

> There is no learning or creativity without the body.
>
> — the workshop slides, after Kay Louise Aldred, *Embodied Education*

So regulation is not a luxury. It is a [human need](https://stimpunks.org/glossary/human-needs/) — the ongoing work of finding enough balance to engage, connect and create.

**This is not a scale.** Nobody is rated, nobody is checked in on, and there is no state a person is supposed to get back to. The deck draws that line too: it is not against regulation, it refuses the tool that turns regulation into a chart with names on it. This workshop shows the other way. It changes the room and the company, and it leaves the person alone.

## Three places, and moving between them

The workshop worked with three of the [primordial learning spaces](https://stimpunks.org/glossary/caves-campfires-watering-holes/). Each one is somewhere to be, with somebody or with nobody, and each supports a different state of a nervous system.

- **The campfire** — together, with one person framing it. Shared meaning and orientation: a story, a frame, everybody knowing what is happening and what comes next. That predictability lowers uncertainty and cognitive load, which is why it can regulate. Being present asks for very little.
- **The watering hole** — with peers, exchanging. Conversation, body doubling, shared interests, stimming together, quiet company. Social, without being too much.
- **The cave** — alone. Solitude and sensory autonomy: rest, quiet, deep focus, withdrawal without stigma and without explanation. For many Neurodivergent people a cave is not an extra. It is a condition of being well.

**Regulation is not staying in one of them. It is being free to move between them.** When a culture makes room for all three, people can handle more, and learning, creativity and connection have room to grow.

A Cavendish Space has two more places, the library and the habitat. [What is a Cavendish Space?](space.html) has all five, and [Zone a room](rooms.html) checks a room of your own against them.

## Ways of connecting are ways of regulating

At the watering hole, the workshop named [Neurodivergent love languages](https://stimpunks.org/2022/01/22/the-five-neurodivergent-love-languages-2/) as co-regulation:

- info-dumping
- parallel play and body doubling
- sharing special interests
- stimming together
- deep presence without pressure
- communicating in whatever way works for you

> Cavendish Space recognises these as forms of care, not problems to correct.
>
> — Helen Edgar, the workshop slides

Connection has often come to Neurodivergent people with correction attached, or with pressure to perform. Over time that wears safety down, and what is left is masking and exhaustion. Helen puts the question underneath all of it plainly:

> Do we feel safe with ourselves, and do we feel safe with others?
>
> — Helen Edgar, on stimpunks.org

When these ways of being together are honored, safety goes up. When safety goes up, so does capacity — for trust, for learning, for joy.

## How the workshop ran

It moved through the places it was about:

1. **Campfire** — community safety, and learning about Cavendish Space.
2. **Watering hole** — a sensory activity, together.
3. **Cave** — time alone to regulate.
4. **Watering hole** — sharing what people noticed.
5. **Campfire** — what we take away, and closing.

### At the watering hole: make a sensory sock ball

First, play with the fillings. Run your fingers through dried lentils. Crunch popcorn. Smell warm cherry stones. Then make one:

1. Find a clean sock. Any color, any pattern.
2. Stretch it over a cup and roll it down.
3. Choose a filling: dry rice or pasta, lentils, pebbles, beads, or anything else you like.
4. Fill it to a size that feels comfy.
5. If you want, add a few drops of a scent.
6. Tie it off with an elastic band or string.
7. If you want, trim the extra sock.
8. If you want, decorate it — a ribbon works.

Some people chatted, some typed, some stayed quiet, some moved around. Some followed the picture instructions, and some made theirs alongside somebody else on video. Being there was the practice.

### Then, a choice

Stay at the watering hole, or go to your own cave for ten minutes. Both are right.

In the cave, do what feels right. Change the light, get a blanket, stim, put music on. Explore the sock ball with one sense or several — smell it, feel it, tap it, roll it, squeeze it, hold it against a different part of you. Or do something else entirely. Sit, lie down, or jump around.

Nobody had to be productive, responsive, or visible.

### Back at the campfire

People gathered what they had felt and noticed. Nobody analyzed it, and nobody performed it.

- What felt good for your sensory system?
- Which place worked best for you today?
- Could Cavendish Space work for you, or for the people around you?

## Running one with the deck

The same five steps work with the deck on the table. [Lay a spread](deck.html) at the first campfire, and nobody has to say a word — a card turned up is a turn, and a card left face-down stays private.

What the workshop offered is what some of the What helps cards ask for: *busy hands*, *let me stim*, *parallel existence*, *let me come and go*, *another way to talk*. The lily pad *coming back* is the cave break said out loud — hold my place, I will return.

The closing questions are about the room, not the people in it. If the answers become changes, write them down as changes to the room. [Hold a cogen](cogens.html) is one way to make that plan with the people who will use it.

## Where the idea comes from

Ryan Boren named Cavendish Space after reading Steve Silberman's *NeuroTribes*, and the life of Henry Cavendish it tells — a scientist who flourished because his surroundings let him withdraw, work alone and think his own way, with a great deal of privilege behind that, which the workshop named. The turn was Silberman's question: not what is wrong with this person, but what this person needs from their environment. Helen and Ryan carried it into a joint chapter on Neuroqueer learning spaces for Nick Walker's forthcoming [*Neuroqueer Theory and Practice*](https://neuroqueer.com/). There is a Stimpunks chapter on it in [*Sensory Spaces: An A–Z of Immersive and Creative Environments to Support Sensory Integration*](https://www.routledge.com/Sensory-Spaces-An-A-Z-of-Immersive-and-Creative-Environments-to-Support-Sensory-Integration/Navin/p/book/9781032989112) too. [Origin & lineage](origin.html) has the longer story, and [Relational Pattern Languages and Indigenous Influence](https://stimpunks.org/projects/cavendish-space-project/relational-pattern-languages-and-indigenous-influence/) is a note on influences.
