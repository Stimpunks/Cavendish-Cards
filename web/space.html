Cavendish Space

What is a Cavendish Space?

Psychologically and sensory safe space suited to deep work, flow states, intermittent collaboration, and collaborative niche construction.

Most environments are not built for us. Schools, workplaces, and public spaces are designed around a norm that excludes Neurodivergent and Disabled people by default — too loud, too crowded, too socially demanding, too rigid about how and when you take part. Cavendish Space is the answer to that. It names what we need and gives a framework for building it, wherever we are, with whatever we have.

The shortest version: you meet a need by shaping the surroundings, not by changing the person.

This is the condensed account, written for the two tools on this site. The full one — with the sources, the research, and the argument at length — lives at stimpunks.org, and it is the canonical version. Everything below is drawn from it; where the two differ, that one is right.

Who Cavendish was, and why the name

Henry Cavendish discovered hydrogen, weighed the Earth, and was by any modern reading Autistic. He worked alone, at home, on his own terms. His social anxiety was severe enough that his peers at the Royal Society learned to address him sideways — “the way to talk to Cavendish is never to look at him, but to talk as it were into a vacancy.”

The point of the story is not the eccentricity. It is the estate.

Cavendish could build the conditions he needed because he was wealthy. His house was his laboratory, his sanctuary, his niche. Had his father been a brakeman or a miner, one of the great scientists of the age would likely have ended his life on a ward at Bedlam — and the science would simply not exist.

So the name carries an argument: the conditions that let a mind work should not require nobility. We build Cavendish Space so that what privilege bought for one man is available to everyone else.

The concept, and the page it comes from, are dedicated to Steve Silberman (1957–2024), whose NeuroTribes put Cavendish in front of us. May his memory be a revolution.

Caves, campfires, and watering holes

Three shapes the niche takes. Not metaphors — actual spaces, actual rhythms, actual needs. They come from David Thornburg's primordial learning metaphors, and Cavendish's own life ran on all three: his house, the Royal Society Monday Club, and a colleague who carried his ideas out into the world for him.

The cave

A private space to think, reflect, and turn external knowledge into internal belief. The work that needs solitude and depth. Protect the quiet: don't make company or an explanation the price of it.

The campfire

A space where people gather to learn from someone who knows — the storyteller's circle, where insight passes down and culture replicates. The exchange that needs safety and small scale. A campfire is not a stage.

The watering hole

An informal space where peers share what they are finding out, each of them learner and teacher at once. The connection that needs low stakes and no performance. Being near people is the whole point, and joining in stays optional.

Together they form an ecology. Separately, each one is a lifeline for someone.

The spatial typology has peer-reviewed grounding. McGoldrick et al. (2025) extend the Autistic SPACE framework into schools. They call for compartmentalised zones with clear boundaries, quiet withdrawal spaces, and flexible environments that give Autistic students control over their own sensory and regulation needs. Caves, campfires, and watering holes are the named design system for what that research describes as necessary and leaves unnamed.

Two more places show up in the tools on this site. The library — where what a group has figured out is kept and shared — and the habitat, the whole landscape holding the others: light, sound, texture, and rhythm. They are additions to the three primordial spaces rather than part of them, and you will meet all five in the deck and in zone a room.

The rhythm that keeps them apart

The three spaces only work if moving between them is voluntary. Forced constant contact collapses all three into one overloading environment.

Intermittent collaboration is that rhythm: group work punctuated by breaks to think and work alone. For Neurodivergent people it is not a preference. It is a condition for being able to take part at all.

Cave, campfire, and watering hole moods map onto the red, yellow, and green of interaction badges — the Autistic community's own convention for saying how open you are right now without having to explain it. The deck carries them as a realm of their own.

What a Cavendish Space is made of

Eleven elements. The definitions are the ones we publish.

  • Caves, campfires, and watering holes — the three spaces above.
  • Intermittent collaboration — group work punctuated by breaks to think and work by ourselves.
  • Niche construction — directly modifying the environment so that it improves someone's chances of doing well.
  • Flow states — the experience of complete absorption in the present moment.
  • Sensory safety — understanding the sensing and perceptual world, and being serious about sensory needs in every setting.
  • Psychological safety — feeling included, safe to learn, safe to contribute, and safe to challenge the status quo, without fear of being embarrassed, marginalised, or punished.
  • Learner safety — safety to engage in discovery: to ask, experiment, and make mistakes.
  • Embodiment and regulation — staying present in your own body to sensation, emotion, and surroundings without tipping into dysregulation; and tending to what the body needs.
  • Cognitive liberty — sovereignty over your own mind and your own cognitive processes.
  • Somatic liberty — freedom of embodiment: to adopt, indulge, or experiment with any style of movement, and to give bodily expression to your neurodivergence.
  • Neurological pluralism — many different bodyminds, with diverse and conflicting needs, coexisting peaceably and interdependently.

Psychological and learner safety are after Timothy R. Clark's four stages (2020); cognitive and somatic liberty and neurological pluralism are after Nick Walker's Neuroqueer Heresies (2021); caves, campfires, and watering holes are after David Thornburg (2013).

Dandelions, tulips, and orchids

Psychological safety matters for everyone, but it does not matter equally. People differ substantially in how much the quality of a space, a relationship, or a culture affects their capacity to function. You cannot calibrate an environment without knowing the range of people it has to hold.

  • Dandelions — low sensitivity. Relatively resilient to environmental change, and able to do well in many conditions.
  • Tulips — medium sensitivity. Most people are here.
  • Orchids — high sensitivity. Deeply affected by conditions both bad and good: they struggle more in harmful environments and flourish spectacularly in supportive ones.

The flower metaphor comes from Lionetti et al. (2018) in Translational Psychiatry. The reason it matters here is the last clause: high sensitivity is not only a vulnerability. The same wiring that makes a hostile room unbearable makes a good one transformative. Design for orchids and the dandelions are fine too. Design for dandelions and you lose the orchids entirely.

What it refuses

Cavendish Space is a counter-deficit idea, and it is easier to describe by what it will not do.

  • It does not fix people. If a room is not working, the room is the thing that changes. Broken systems, not broken people.
  • It does not treat access as charity. Access is infrastructure — never optional, never a favour, never a reward for coping well first.
  • It does not score anyone. No assessment, no pass mark, no certificate. Neither the deck nor the room tool will give you a number, and that is deliberate.
  • It does not ask for a diagnosis at the door. A space that only works for people who have been officially labelled is not a safe space; it is a filtered one.
  • It does not pretend needs never conflict. Neurological pluralism means holding diverse and conflicting needs at once. A conflict between two access needs is a design problem, not a contest.
